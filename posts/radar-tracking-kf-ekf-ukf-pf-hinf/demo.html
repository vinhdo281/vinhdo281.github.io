Reading the results

The nonlinearity is in the measurement

The state is (x, y), but the radar measures (r, φ). The measurement function contains a square root and an arctangent, so the EKF's Hk changes with the target position. A 4° bearing error is about 14 m across the line of sight at 200 m range and about 56 m at 800 m, so the measurement error region is an arc, not a circle.

The KF has to use one shared R

The KF converts (r, φ) to (x, y) and uses one fixed, circular R chosen from the mean range of the path. At long range it trusts the measurement too much, at short range too little. The other four filters use (r, φ) directly with the right noise for each, so the EKF, UKF and PF usually end up a few percent below the KF, and more when the bearing noise is large.

The UKF almost matches the EKF here

Over the ranges in this simulation the measurement function bends only gently, so the UKF's sigma points and the EKF's Jacobian give nearly the same error. The UKF pays off when the measurement function bends strongly or is hard to differentiate; in exchange it evaluates the measurement function 9 times per step.

The PF needs enough particles

With 500 particles the PF stays within about 5% of the EKF. With 100 particles its error is about 75% higher, and with 50 it regularly loses the target. Cost grows linearly with the number of particles. Where a PF really wins is a multi-modal or non-Gaussian distribution, which this simulation does not have.

A wrong motion model dominates

On the circle or the weave with q far too small (0.1), every filter lags behind the target, and that lag outweighs any nonlinearity effect. In that case the plain KF is the best of the five, the EKF and UKF are 20–30% worse, and the PF loses the target. Drag q down to 0.1 on the weave to see this, then raise it again.

Linear sensor

With the position (x, y) sensor the measurement is linear: KF, EKF and UKF give identical results. The PF only approximates them because it samples at random, and gets closer with more particles. H∞ equals the KF only at η = 0; with a larger η it is deliberately more conservative.

H∞ is insurance against the worst case

The Kalman filter minimises the mean squared error, provided q and R are right. H∞ needs no noise statistics and bounds the worst case instead, so it is more cautious: the update inflates the covariance by θ and the filter leans on the measurement more than on the model. At the default setting (σ bearing 4°, σ range 10 m, η = 0.1) with q matched, H∞ is 2–7% worse than the EKF. With q too small (q = 0.1 on the circle or the weave) it is clearly better than the EKF: about 55 m vs 66 m on the circle, 76 m vs 89 m on the weave. The plain KF is still slightly better in both cases (53 m and 70 m).

H∞ does not replace tuning q

With the right q (1.3 on the weave) the EKF reaches about 19 m, far better than H∞ with the wrong q (about 76 m). H∞ limits the damage when q is unknown; it does not replace tuning it. θ cannot be raised freely either: the updated covariance must stay positive definite, and beyond that limit the filter diverges. In the setups we tried, η = 0.1 never diverged, while from about 0.12 the harsh setups (very small measurement noise, large q) started to diverge.
