PART 1 Physics & Lagrangian Equations: Why is TWSBR Underactuated?

A Two-Wheeled Self-Balancing Robot (TWSBR) is a classic underactuated, non-minimum phase mechatronic system. Understanding the system's mathematics explains why conventional control fails.

The system possesses 3 mechanical degrees of freedom (chassis tilt angle $\\theta$, wheel angular displacement $\\phi$, and heading yaw $\\psi$), but only two actuators (DC brushed/brushless motors applying torques $\\tau_L, \\tau_R$). The rotational joint between the wheels and the chassis has no direct restoring actuator—it relies entirely on horizontal ground reaction forces generated by the wheels.

Using the Euler-Lagrange formulation, the generalized coordinates are chosen as $q = [x, \\theta]^T$, where $x = R \\phi$ is the linear displacement of the wheel axle and $\\theta$ is the pitch angle from the vertical. The Lagrangian $L = T - V$ is defined by kinetic energy $T$ and potential energy $V$:

$$T = \\frac{1}{2}(M + 2m)\\dot{x}^2 + M L \\dot{x}\\dot{\\theta}\\cos\\theta + \\frac{1}{2}(J_{\\text{chassis}} + M L^2)\\dot{\\theta}^2 + \\frac{1}{2} J_{\\text{wheel}}\\frac{\\dot{x}^2}{R^2}$$

$$V = M g L \\cos\\theta$$

Applying the Euler-Lagrange equation $\\frac{d}{dt}\\left(\\frac{\\partial L}{\\partial \\dot{q}_i}\\right) - \\frac{\\partial L}{\\partial q_i} = Q_i$, the coupled nonlinear equations of motion are:

$$(M + 2m + \\frac{2J_{\\text{wheel}}}{R^2})\\ddot{x} + M L \\ddot{\\theta}\\cos\\theta - M L \\dot{\\theta}^2 \\sin\\theta = \\frac{2\\tau}{R}$$

$$(J_{\\text{chassis}} + M L^2)\\ddot{\\theta} + M L \\ddot{x}\\cos\\theta - M g L \\sin\\theta = -2\\tau$$

Key Physical Insight: Notice the term $M L \\ddot{x}\\cos\\theta$ in the pitch equation. The only way to generate a torque that accelerates the chassis in the direction of vertical equilibrium ($\\ddot{\\theta} < 0$) is by accelerating the wheel base forward ($\\ddot{x} > 0$). Acceleration of the cart directly dictates the restoring torque of the pendulum.

PART 2 Theoretical Dilemma: Why Single-Loop PID Catastrophically Fails

Many beginners attempt to control a self-balancing robot with a single PID controller that tries to regulate position error $e_x = x_{\\text{target}} - x$. This approach is doomed to fail due to non-minimum phase zero dynamics:

  • The Conflict: Suppose the robot is at $x = -0.5\\text{m}$ and needs to move forward to $x = 0$. A single-loop position controller sees a positive error $e_x > 0$ and immediately applies forward motor torque ($\\tau > 0$).
  • The Catastrophe: Forward torque drives the wheels forward ($+\\ddot{x}$). According to Newton's third law and our inertia coupling term, the bottom of the chassis accelerates forward faster than the center of mass, causing the chassis to tilt backward ($-\\theta$).
  • The Inversion: Once tilted backward, gravity accelerates the robot backward, compounding the problem. To travel forward, an inverted pendulum must first pitch forward ($+\\theta$), allowing gravity to pull the center of mass forward, after which the wheels chase the center of mass.
Conclusion: Position error and pitch error demand opposite initial torque directions. A single PID loop cannot resolve this dynamic contradiction. A cascade control structure is mathematically required to separate attitude stabilization from translational displacement.

PART 3 Cascade Architecture & Bandwidth Decoupling (500Hz vs 50Hz)

The cascade control topology decomposes the problem hierarchically into two nested feedback loops running at distinctly decoupled frequencies:

$$\\text{Outer Loop (Position/Velocity) } [50\\text{ Hz}] \\longrightarrow \\theta_{\\text{ref}} \\longrightarrow \\text{Inner Loop (Attitude Balance) } [500\\text{ Hz}] \\longrightarrow \\tau$$

1. Fast Inner Loop (Attitude Stabilization - 500 Hz / $\\Delta t = 2\\text{ms}$):

Governed by high-frequency IMU feedback. Its sole objective is to force pitch angle $\\theta$ to track a commanded reference angle $\\theta_{\\text{ref}}$:

$$\\tau(t) = K_p^\\theta (\\theta_{\\text{ref}} - \\theta) - K_d^\\theta \\dot{\\theta}$$

Why no integral term ($K_i^\\theta$) in the inner loop? An integrator on pitch introduces phase lag ($90^\\circ$ phase penalty), which destabilizes marginally stable inverted systems. Steady-state pitch bias is instead handled by the outer loop.

2. Slow Outer Loop (Position & Velocity Regulation - 50 Hz / $\\Delta t = 20\\text{ms}$):

Governed by wheel optical encoder odometry. Compares target position $x_{\\text{ref}}$ against cart position $x$, outputting the required lean angle $\\theta_{\\text{ref}}$:

$$\\theta_{\\text{ref}}(t) = K_p^x (x_{\\text{ref}} - x) + K_i^x \\int (x_{\\text{ref}} - x) dt - K_d^x \\dot{x}$$

Why the $10\\times$ Bandwidth Ratio Matters: The inner loop bandwidth must be at least 5 to 10 times higher than the outer loop. This ensures the inner balance dynamics settle so quickly that the outer position loop can mathematically treat the inner loop as an instantaneous tilt actuator with transfer function $\\frac{\\theta(s)}{\\theta_{\\text{ref}}(s)} \\approx 1$.

PART 4 Detailed MATLAB / Simulink Nonlinear Plant Modeling

The Simulink model Simulation_TWSBR.slx contains complete non-linear dynamics, actuator saturation limits, and physical friction losses:

% Parameters definition script (init_twsbr.m)
M   = 1.250;      % Chassis mass (kg)
m   = 0.160;      % Single wheel mass (kg)
R   = 0.033;      % Wheel radius (m)
L   = 0.082;      % Distance from wheel axle to Center of Mass (m)
J_c = 0.0125;     % Chassis pitch moment of inertia (kg*m^2)
J_w = 0.00012;    % Wheel rotational inertia (kg*m^2)
g   = 9.810;      % Gravitational acceleration (m/s^2)

% Motor Electrical & Mechanical Constants
Km  = 0.045;      % Motor torque constant (Nm/A)
Ke  = 0.045;      % Back-EMF constant (V/(rad/s))
Ra  = 3.200;      % Motor armature winding resistance (Ohms)
Vmax= 12.0;       % H-bridge supply voltage limit (V)

% Saturation clamp on reference tilt angle to prevent tip-over:
theta_max_deg = 12.0; % Max allowable tilt command (degrees)

Actuator Saturation Modeling: Real DC motors cannot deliver infinite torque. In Simulink, the motor voltage is clamped at $\\pm 12\\text{V}$. When the motor reaches maximum RPM, back-EMF ($V_{\\text{emf}} = K_e \\omega$) cancels the supply voltage, causing delivered torque $\\tau = \\frac{K_m (V - K_e \\omega)}{R_a}$ to drop to zero. Modeling this prevents unrealistically optimistic control gains.

PART 5 Line-by-Line ESP32 FreeRTOS Firmware Implementation

The embedded C++ firmware leverages the ESP32 dual-core Xtensa LX6 architecture under FreeRTOS. Core 1 is pinned exclusively to high-priority hard real-time control, while Core 0 handles WiFi telemetry and Bluetooth user commands.

// ============================================================================
// Core 1 Real-Time Balance Interrupt Task (Fixed 500 Hz / 2000 microsecond loop)
// ============================================================================
void BalanceTask(void *pvParameters) {
    TickType_t xLastWakeTime = xTaskGetTickCount();
    const TickType_t xFrequency = pdMS_TO_TICKS(2); // 2ms = 500 Hz

    for(;;) {
        // Step 1: Read raw IMU data via hardware I2C at 400kHz
        int16_t ax_raw, ay_raw, az_raw;
        int16_t gx_raw, gy_raw, gz_raw;
        mpu.getMotion6(&ax_raw, &ay_raw, &az_raw, &gx_raw, &gy_raw, &gz_raw);

        // Step 2: Convert to physical SI units (deg/s and rad)
        float gyro_y_rate = (float)gy_raw / 131.0f; // Scale factor for +/- 250 deg/s range
        float accel_angle = atan2f((float)ax_raw, (float)az_raw) * 57.2957795f; // Rad to Deg

        // Step 3: Complementary Sensor Fusion Filter (Alpha = 0.98)
        float dt = 0.002f; // 2 milliseconds
        pitch_angle = 0.98f * (pitch_angle + gyro_y_rate * dt) + 0.02f * accel_angle;

        // Step 4: Outer loop execution decimation (every 10 cycles = 50 Hz)
        loop_counter++;
        if (loop_counter >= 10) {
            loop_counter = 0;
            // Read quadrature wheel encoders
            long enc_left = encoder_left.getCount();
            long enc_right = encoder_right.getCount();
            cart_position = ((enc_left + enc_right) * 0.5f) * METERS_PER_TICK;
            cart_velocity = (cart_position - prev_cart_position) / 0.020f; // 50 Hz velocity
            prev_cart_position = cart_position;

            // Outer Position PID computation
            float pos_error = target_position - cart_position;
            pos_integral += pos_error * 0.020f;
            // Anti-Windup Clamping on Position Integrator:
            pos_integral = constrain(pos_integral, -0.3f, 0.3f);

            // Compute commanded reference pitch angle
            theta_ref = (Kp_pos * pos_error) + (Ki_pos * pos_integral) - (Kd_pos * cart_velocity);
            // CRITICAL: Clamp commanded angle to +/- 12 degrees
            theta_ref = constrain(theta_ref, -12.0f, +12.0f);
        }

        // Step 5: Fast Inner Attitude PID computation
        float angle_error = theta_ref - pitch_angle;
        float torque_output = (Kp_angle * angle_error) - (Kd_angle * gyro_y_rate);

        // Step 6: Convert torque command to PWM with deadzone compensation
        apply_motor_pwm(torque_output);

        // Enforce strict deterministic real-time scheduling
        vTaskDelayUntil(&xLastWakeTime, xFrequency);
    }
}

PART 6 Complementary Filter Derivation: Why Alpha = 0.98?

Why do we choose $\\alpha = 0.98$ in $\\theta_t = 0.98(\\theta_{t-1} + \\dot{\\theta}_{\\text{gyro}}\\Delta t) + 0.02\\theta_{\\text{accel}}$? This is not an arbitrary guess—it is mathematically derived from the sensor noise crossover frequency:

  • Accelerometer weakness: Measures true gravity vector when stationary, but is corrupted by high-frequency linear translational accelerations and motor vibrations ($> 5\\text{ Hz}$).
  • Gyroscope weakness: Extremely responsive and immune to linear vibration, but subject to low-frequency DC bias drift ($< 0.5\\text{ Hz}$).
  • Filter Time Constant Equation: $$\\tau = \\frac{\\alpha \\cdot \\Delta t}{1 - \\alpha} \\iff \\alpha = \\frac{\\tau}{\\tau + \\Delta t}$$
  • Crossover Cutoff Frequency: $$f_c = \\frac{1}{2\\pi \\tau}$$
  • With sampling interval $\\Delta t = 0.002\\text{ s}$ and chosen time constant $\\tau \\approx 0.10\\text{ s}$ ($f_c \\approx 1.6\\text{ Hz}$): $$\\alpha = \\frac{0.10}{0.10 + 0.002} = \\frac{0.10}{0.102} \\approx 0.9804$$

Thus, $\\alpha = 0.98$ guarantees that all signals above $1.6\\text{ Hz}$ are governed by clean rate gyroscope integration, while slow DC drift below $1.6\\text{ Hz}$ is continuously corrected by gravity accelerometer vectors.

PART 7 Systematic Gain Tuning Protocol & Anti-Windup Strategy

Tuning must follow an invariant, methodical sequence:

  1. Zero Everything Out: Set $K_p^x = 0, K_i^x = 0, K_d^x = 0, K_p^\\theta = 0, K_d^\\theta = 0$. Hold the robot by hand.
  2. Tune Inner $K_p^\\theta$ (Proportional Balance): Gradually raise $K_p^\\theta$. When disturbed, the wheels should immediately drive underneath the chassis to restore balance. Keep raising until the robot balances briefly by itself and begins a gentle, low-frequency oscillation.
  3. Tune Inner $K_d^\\theta$ (Derivative Damping): Slowly increase $K_d^\\theta$. This adds damping torque against rapid angular velocities ($\\dot{\\theta}$). The oscillation will vanish, and the robot will feel rigid and stiff when pushed manually.
  4. Tune Outer $K_d^x$ (Velocity Drag): Turn on outer velocity damping. This prevents the robot from drifting across the room; if it moves forward, $K_d^x \\dot{x}$ commands a backward pitch to slow down.
  5. Tune Outer $K_p^x$ & $K_i^x$ (Position Lock): Add $K_p^x$ to pull the robot back to its starting coordinate. Introduce small $K_i^x$ with strict integral clamping (Anti-Windup) to eliminate steady-state offsets caused by chassis weight imbalance.

PART 8 Engineering Diagnostics & Common Failure Modes

Diagnostic Failure 1: Robot vigorously shakes / jitters at 50Hz+:
Root Cause: $K_d^\\theta$ gain is too high, or raw gyroscope noise is being amplified by differentiation. Solution: Lower $K_d^\\theta$ by 30% or add a 2nd-order low-pass filter on gyro readings.
Diagnostic Failure 2: Robot slowly accelerates in one direction until falling:
Root Cause: Mechanical center of mass is offset from vertical zero, or accelerometer was not calibrated horizontally on a precision level table. Solution: Adjust software zero-point trim $\\theta_{\\text{offset}} = +1.2^\\circ$.
Diagnostic Failure 3: Robot falls over when pushed firmly (Integrator Windup):
Root Cause: While pushed, position error accumulates in $pos\_integral$, demanding tilt angles $> 20^\\circ$ that exceed wheel traction limits. Solution: Enforce anti-windup clamping on $\\theta_{\\text{ref}} \\in [-12^\\circ, +12^\\circ]$ and freeze integration when error exceeds $0.2\\text{m}$.

TEIL 1 Physik & Lagrange-Gleichungen: Warum ist das System unteraktuiert?

Ein zweirädriger Balancierroboter (TWSBR) ist ein unteraktuiertes mechatronisches System mit nicht-minimaler Phase. Es besitzt 3 mechanische Freiheitsgrade (Kippwinkel $\\theta$, Radwinkel $\\phi$, Gierwinkel $\\psi$), wird jedoch nur von zwei Motoren angetrieben. Das Kippgelenk zwischen Rädern und Chassis besitzt keinen eigenen Aktuator und muss über horizontale Radreaktionskräfte stabilisiert werden.

Über den Euler-Lagrange-Ansatz mit verallgemeinerten Koordinaten $q = [x, \\theta]^T$ ergeben sich die gekoppelten Bewegungsgleichungen:

$$(M + 2m + \\frac{2J_{\\text{wheel}}}{R^2})\\ddot{x} + M L \\ddot{\\theta}\\cos\\theta - M L \\dot{\\theta}^2 \\sin\\theta = \\frac{2\\tau}{R}$$

$$(J_{\\text{chassis}} + M L^2)\\ddot{\\theta} + M L \\ddot{x}\\cos\\theta - M g L \\sin\\theta = -2\\tau$$

Physikalische Koppelung: Der Term $M L \\ddot{x}\\cos\\theta$ zeigt: Ein aufrichtendes Moment ($\\ddot{\\theta} < 0$) kann nur durch Beschleunigung der Radachse ($\\ddot{x} > 0$) erzeugt werden.

TEIL 2 Theoretisches Dilemma: Warum einkreisige PID-Regler versagen

Ein einkreisiger PID-Regler zur Positionsregelung $e_x = x_{\\text{soll}} - x > 0$ gibt sofort Vorwärtsmoment aus. Dadurch beschleunigt die Achse nach vorne und das Chassis kippt durch Trägheit nach hinten ($-\\theta$), wodurch der Roboter unweigerlich umstürzt. Um vorwärts zu fahren, muss der Roboter sich zuerst nach vorne neigen ($+\\theta$), damit die Schwerkraft ihn zieht. Positions- und Neigungsfehler fordern entgegengesetzte Stellgrößen – ein einzelner Regelkreis kann diesen Konflikt nicht lösen.

TEIL 3 Kaskadenarchitektur & Bandbreitenentkopplung (500Hz vs 50Hz)

Die Kaskadenregelung trennt die Dynamik strikt in zwei Frequenzen:

  • Schneller Innenkreis (500 Hz / $\\Delta t = 2\\text{ms}$): Stabilisiert den Neigungswinkel $\\theta$ auf den Sollwinkel $\\theta_{\\text{ref}}$: $\\tau = K_p^\\theta (\\theta_{\\text{ref}} - \\theta) - K_d^\\theta \\dot{\\theta}$.
  • Langsamer Außenkreis (50 Hz / $\\Delta t = 20\\text{ms}$): Berechnet aus der Positionsabweichung den erforderlichen Soll-Neigungswinkel $\\theta_{\\text{ref}} = K_p^x (x_{\\text{ref}} - x) + K_i^x \\int e_x dt - K_d^x \\dot{x}$.
Bandbreitenverhältnis 10:1: Da der Innenkreis zehnmal schneller reagiert, sieht der Außenkreis die Neigungsregelung als nahezu verzögerungsfreien Stellantrieb.

TEIL 6 Komplementärfilter-Herleitung: Warum Alpha = 0.98?

Aus der Filterzeitkonstante $\\tau = \\frac{\\alpha \\Delta t}{1 - \\alpha}$ folgt bei $\\Delta t = 2\\text{ms}$ und $\\tau = 0.1\\text{s}$ exakt $\\alpha = \\frac{0.1}{0.102} \\approx 0.98$. Frequenzen oberhalb von 1,6 Hz werden vom rauscharmen Gyroskop getragen, während die langsame Drift unterhalb von 1,6 Hz durch den Schwerkraftvektor des Beschleunigungsmessers korrigiert wird.

TEIL 7 Systematisches Tuning-Protokoll & Anti-Windup-Strategie

Reihenfolge: 1. $K_p^\\theta$ erhöhen bis zum leichten Schwingen. 2. $K_d^\\theta$ erhöhen für aperiodische Dämpfung. 3. $K_d^x$ für Geschwindigkeitsdämpfung. 4. $K_p^x$ mit harter Begrenzung $\\theta_{\\text{ref}} \\in [-12^\\circ, +12^\\circ]$ gegen Umkippen.

TEIL 8 Technische Fehlerdiagnose & Häufige Ursachen

Hochfrequentes Zittern deutet auf zu hohes $K_d^\\theta$ hin. Kontinuierliches Wegdriften erfordert einen mechanischen Nullpunkt-Offset-Abgleich $\\theta_{\\text{offset}}$.
